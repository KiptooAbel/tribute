A curious boy from Western Kenya

Elly Savatia grew up in Western Kenya, fascinated by how things worked. From about the age of ten he was taking apart radios, clocks and other household electronics just to see what was inside. That habit of looking closely at a problem became the foundation of everything he has built since.

Before the COVID-19 pandemic he ran robotics workshops in schools across Kenya. During one of those visits to a high school in Western Kenya, he came face to face with the communication barriers faced by deaf learners. It was a turning point: he decided to use technology to close that gap.

Building technology with the deaf community

Together with his friend and co-founder Emmanuel Mulengwa, Elly first developed Veezaviz, a mobile application that interprets hand movements into text for people who are deaf. That early work grew into Signvrse, a social enterprise he founded in 2020 and leads as Founder and CEO.

Signvrse uses artificial intelligence and 3D animation to turn speech and text into lifelike sign language avatars. Its flagship product, Terp 360, translates spoken language into sign language in real time. The avatars are trained on sign language data recorded locally, so they reflect how people in Africa actually sign.

“You don’t build solutions and take them to the people, you build with them.” Elly Savatia, speaking about how Signvrse works

Milestones

  1. 2020 Founds Signvrse and is recognised among young leaders by the We Are Family Foundation.
  2. 2021 Veezaviz, the app he built with Emmanuel Mulengwa, gains attention across Africa.
  3. 2023 Signvrse is featured among the Commonwealth Innovation Awards winners.
  4. 16 October 2025 Wins the Royal Academy of Engineering’s Africa Prize for Engineering Innovation, with £50,000 to scale Terp 360. He pitched to a live audience and a panel of seven judges.

Why I admire him

As a young software developer in Nairobi, I find Elly’s story deeply encouraging. He did not wait for permission, funding or a perfect setting. He started with curiosity, noticed a real problem in his own community, and kept building until the solution reached the world stage.

What I take from his journey:

  • Technology is most powerful when it includes the people who are usually left out.
  • The best solutions are built with the community, not just for it.
  • Young Africans can compete and lead at the highest level of engineering.
  • Curiosity, practised daily, is a career plan.

Thank you, Elly, for showing that code can give people a voice.
